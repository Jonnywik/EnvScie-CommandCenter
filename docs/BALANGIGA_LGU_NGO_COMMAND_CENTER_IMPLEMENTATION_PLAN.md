# Balangiga LGU and NGO Command Center Implementation Plan

**Purpose.** This plan explains how the Code for Resilience Command Center can be introduced as an LGU-owned disaster-risk-reduction capability in Balangiga, Eastern Samar, while connecting NGOs, civil-society organizations (CSOs), barangays, and humanitarian partners through accountable and privacy-respecting arrangements.

**Planning position.** The Command Center should begin as a **municipal pilot and preparedness tool**, not a promise of instant emergency response. It must remain under the lawful authority of the Balangiga LGU’s disaster-risk-reduction structure. NGOs and CSOs should strengthen community preparedness, anticipatory action, training, referral capacity, and resource coordination; they must not replace LGU authority over public warnings, dispatch, or access to identifiable SOS records.

> **Core rule:** A partner connection is a governed working relationship, not an automatic system login. No NGO should receive unrestricted access to resident location, health, SOS, or household data.

---

## 1. Local Planning Context

Balangiga is a **third-class municipality** in Eastern Samar with a reported 2024 POPCEN population of **14,473**. Eastern Samar’s PSGC record identifies 22 municipalities, one city, and 597 barangays, which reinforces the need for provincial and inter-municipal coordination while keeping the first rollout modest and locally manageable.[1]

The plan must also recognize affordability and access constraints. In 2023, PSA RSSO VIII reported poverty incidence among families in Eastern Samar at **24.7%** and poverty incidence among the provincial population at **33.4%**. These provincial indicators do not substitute for a Balangiga household survey, but they strongly argue against designs that depend on paid subscriptions, high-end devices, continuous broadband, or costly travel for community participation.[2]

Balangiga has a history of community-oriented flood-control activity, and recent reporting identifies Balangiga among Eastern Samar LGUs involved in anticipatory-action work with Good Neighbors Philippines; the cited reasons include exposure to typhoons, storm surge, and flooding.[3] [4] This makes a locally validated, barangay-aware early-warning and coordination model more appropriate than a generic city-scale control room.

| Local condition | Planning implication | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Municipal scale: 14,473 people | Do not overbuild a 24/7 city-style center at the start. | Begin with a small LGU core team, part-time duty roster, and selected barangay focal points. |
| Coastal and multi-hazard exposure | Early warning, evacuation readiness, and field communication are central use cases. | Prioritize verified alerts, centers, SOS intake, risk context, and drill-supported workflows. |
| Provincial poverty and uneven access | Household costs and high-bandwidth assumptions would exclude people. | Keep basic resident access free, support offline content and SMS handoff, and offer assisted onboarding. |
| Community participation history | Residents and local organizations should help shape readiness processes. | Create a structured CSO and barangay engagement process rather than treating communities as data sources only. |
| LGU decision environment | Leadership, council action, staffing, and budget cycles may change over time. | Use position-based policies, written approvals, public criteria, and an auditable handover process. |

### Economic interpretation

Eastern Samar’s reported provincial economic growth should be treated as a **contextual opportunity**, not as evidence that Balangiga has a ready municipal budget, staffing pool, or stable connectivity for a full-scale deployment.[5] The implementation team should first establish a local baseline covering available personnel, current DRRM plan allocations, devices, power backup, connectivity, evacuation-center update processes, and recurrent operating costs. No live-service launch should depend on an NGO grant that cannot be sustained by the LGU after a project ends.

### Political and institutional interpretation

This plan treats “political” considerations as **institutional continuity, lawful authority, transparency, and community trust**—not party affiliation or electoral advantage. The Command Center should be governed by municipal positions and written rules, not individual personalities. Public-warning authority, approval thresholds, partner selection, and access controls must be documented and reviewable by appropriate LGU bodies.

Republic Act No. 10121 calls for a holistic and participatory DRRM approach, local capacity, transparency, accountability, and the participation of CSOs, private actors, volunteers, and communities.[6] This supports NGO inclusion, but it also means that the LGU must lead the system within its lawful DRRM mandate.

## 2. Target Operating Model

### 2.1 Ownership and decision rights

The LGU should create a **Command Center Steering and Data Governance Group** by written municipal action. Its first job is not to buy technology. Its first job is to define authority, operating rules, data boundaries, financing, and readiness gates.

| Role | Recommended holder | Primary responsibility | Must not delegate without written authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Mayor or formally designated municipal official | Ensures policy alignment, inter-office commitment, and accountability. | Emergency declarations, legally reserved executive decisions. |
| System owner | Municipal DRRMO / MDRRMO | Owns operating procedures, duty roster, drills, incident workflow, and readiness reporting. | Public-warning approval or dispatch authority unless formally designated. |
| Operations lead | On-duty DRRM coordinator | Runs the Command Center during exercises or activation, triages incoming information, and logs decisions. | Independent verification or dispatch decisions outside the approved SOP. |
| Public-information authority | Designated LGU public-information and DRRM approvers | Approves public bulletins after source verification and required authorization. | Outsourcing public-alert approval to NGOs or vendors. |
| Data-protection lead | LGU-designated privacy focal person / data protection officer | Maintains data inventory, access matrix, agreements, retention schedule, and breach process. | Sharing case data informally through chat groups. |
| Technical custodian | LGU IT focal person with contracted support if needed | Manages devices, accounts, backups, uptime monitoring, and change control. | Altering operational rules without DRRMO approval. |
| Community and barangay liaison | Barangay focal-point coordinator | Organizes barangay validation, onboarding, feedback, and inclusive participation. | Exposing sensitive SOS data to community volunteers. |

### 2.2 A realistic first operating posture

The initial configuration should use **three levels of operation** rather than claim 24/7 continuous staffing.

| Posture | When used | Staffing model | Command Center activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preparedness | Normal conditions | Part-time MDRRMO owner plus named alternates. | Update centers, test data sources, conduct training, review readiness. |
| Heightened monitoring | Official forecasts or local risk triggers warrant closer attention. | Duty officer plus designated information and barangay focal points. | Review verified updates, check center readiness, confirm contact lists, begin structured logging. |
| Incident coordination | LGU activation or authorized emergency workflow. | Designated incident commander, operations, information, and support roles. | Triage reports, coordinate verified decisions, record actions, provide approved status updates. |

This posture supports continuity during leadership changes, staff leave, power loss, and budget fluctuations. It also makes clear that a dashboard is not the same as a staffed emergency operations center.

### 2.3 Political and institutional safeguards

The following safeguards reduce the risk that the system is viewed as a personal, partisan, or opaque project:

| Risk | Safeguard | Evidence that it is working |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership turnover | Adopt a municipal operating charter, named alternates, system handover checklist, and role-based—not person-based—accounts. | A new office holder can identify authorities, open items, and current procedures without relying on one person. |
| Unequal partner influence | Publish partner-selection criteria, conflict-of-interest declarations, and each partner’s limited role. | NGO offers are evaluated against the same written criteria. |
| Politicized alerts or aid visibility | Require source verification, approval logs, and neutral public messaging rules. | Every bulletin links to a source, approver, scope, and timestamp. |
| Barangay exclusion | Use a documented focal-point selection and feedback process that includes women, youth, older persons, PWDs, farmers, fishers, and other locally relevant groups. | Pilot feedback comes from more than the town proper or one organization. |
| Procurement or vendor dependence | Use documented requirements, acceptance tests, training materials, and an LGU-owned asset/account register. | The LGU can operate essential functions even if a supplier contract changes. |

## 3. NGO and CSO Partnership Framework

### 3.1 Partnership principle

Partner organizations should be connected through **purpose-specific collaboration lanes**, not given one broad “NGO access” role. The plan recommends a two-track approach:

1. **Governance and representation track.** Local CSOs should be considered through the LGU’s lawful accreditation and local-special-body processes. DILG publishes guidelines on CSO accreditation and selection of representatives to local special bodies; Balangiga should confirm the current local implementation process with DILG and its legal office.[7]
2. **Operational delivery track.** NGOs, humanitarian organizations, and private-sector partners should participate under written memoranda of understanding (MoUs), work plans, and—when personal data is involved—data-sharing or data-processing agreements.

### 3.2 Partnership portfolio

| Partner type | Potential contribution | Command Center connection | Data boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barangay organizations and community groups | Local validation, inclusion mapping, preparedness outreach, feedback. | Barangay readiness forms, center verification workflow, training attendance, non-identifiable trend summaries. | No access to individual SOS, health, or household incident records. |
| Good Neighbors Philippines or similar anticipatory-action partner | Forecast-based action design, vulnerable-group inclusion, community preparedness, project learning. | Aggregated trigger dashboards, readiness milestones, pre-agreed action checklists. | No raw resident case feed; case referrals only through approved LGU workflow. |
| Philippine Red Cross / health and humanitarian partners | First aid, volunteer preparedness, referral protocols, emergency medical and blood-service coordination. | Training calendar, capability directory, non-identifiable readiness and request summaries. | Individual data only when a lawful, documented referral is required. |
| Provincial and national government partners | Provincial DRRM coordination, social protection, health, weather, safety, and public-order functions. | Official information feeds, coordination contacts, escalation notes, shared incident formats where authorized. | Use government-to-government rules and the minimum data needed for the task. |
| Private-sector resilience partners | Connectivity, logistics, training support, facilities, or technical mentoring. | Resource-offer registry, service-level commitments, readiness drills. | No resident data access by default. |

The Good Neighbors Philippines connection is especially suitable for early outreach because public reporting identifies Balangiga as a partner municipality in an Eastern Samar anticipatory-action initiative.[3] The plan should nevertheless begin with a **discovery meeting**, not a presumption that the organization will fund, operate, or receive data from the Command Center.

Philippine Red Cross offers another relevant model. Its documented Eastern Samar cooperation discussion with Borongan City covered first aid, emergency medical services, disaster preparedness and response, and blood services, while emphasizing that effective response depends on organizations working together according to their different mandates.[8]

### 3.3 Standard NGO onboarding sequence

Every prospective NGO or CSO should follow the same sequence:

| Step | Decision | Required output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Capability and legitimacy check | Is the organization registered/recognized, locally accepted, relevant to DRRM, and able to protect data? | Partner due-diligence record. |
| 2. Problem fit | Which specific local gap can the partner help address: training, centers, anticipatory action, health referral, logistics, or inclusion? | One-page problem-and-role statement. |
| 3. Scope and authority | What can the partner do, see, request, and escalate? What can it never approve? | Role matrix and escalation map. |
| 4. Data and safeguarding review | Is any personal or sensitive information needed? Can the outcome be achieved with aggregated data instead? | Data-minimization assessment; agreement if required. |
| 5. Pilot work plan | What will be tested, in which barangays, by whom, during which exercise? | Time-bound MoU annex and drill plan. |
| 6. After-action review | Did the partnership improve readiness without privacy, neutrality, or authority failures? | Joint review, lessons, renewal or exit decision. |

### 3.4 Minimum contents of an NGO MoU

Each MoU should contain the following sections: defined purpose; scope of work; named focal points; service areas and beneficiary criteria; decision and escalation roles; resource commitments; training and safeguarding standards; data categories and access rules; retention and disposal; confidentiality; audit/logging; communications protocol; dispute and incident management; term; review; and termination/exit responsibilities.

## 4. Data Governance and System Access

The LGU should act as the **personal information controller** for Command Center data. The Data Privacy Act requires transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality, and it treats health information and political affiliations among sensitive personal information categories.[9] A disaster purpose may be lawful, but it does not justify unlimited collection, distribution, or retention.

### 4.1 Access model

| Access tier | Example users | Information available | Information excluded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 0: Public | Residents, public website visitors | Approved public bulletins, verified alerts, public center information. | SOS details, resident location, response-team details, internal notes. |
| Tier 1: Community readiness | Barangay focal points, accredited community partners | Center readiness tasks, training status, aggregated risk and needs trends. | Identifiable SOS, health, family, and sensitive incident data. |
| Tier 2: Partner coordination | Approved NGO/PRC/provincial focal points | Role-specific resource requests, aggregated dashboards, approved referrals. | Raw case lists unless a documented referral and lawful need exist. |
| Tier 3: LGU operations | Designated MDRRMO, authorized health/social-welfare and incident staff | Incident records and resident-safe operational data needed for their task. | Information beyond a user’s role, unrelated cases, or unrestricted exports. |
| Tier 4: System administration | Designated technical and privacy leads | Technical logs and controlled administration views. | Routine operational case browsing without role justification. |

### 4.2 Non-negotiable controls

1. Use named accounts and least-privilege access; do not share one password across an office or organization.
2. Use pseudonymous mobile installation identifiers where a named identity is unnecessary.
3. Keep public alerts separate from case-management data.
4. Do not send screenshots, live SOS data, health information, or household addresses through unapproved group chats.
5. Log access, status changes, dispatch confirmations, exports, and partner referrals.
6. Require written data-sharing agreements before recurring data exchange with a non-LGU organization.
7. Define retention and deletion periods with the LGU privacy focal person and legal advisers.
8. Conduct tabletop breach exercises and document escalation contacts before a live pilot.

## 5. Phased Implementation Roadmap

The roadmap is deliberately staged. A phase should not advance merely because time has passed; it should advance only when its decision gate is met.

### Phase 0 — Mandate, baseline, and co-design (Weeks 0–6)

**Objective.** Establish lawful ownership, confirm the actual municipal baseline, and agree on a small pilot scope.

| Workstream | Actions | Outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Governance | Form the Steering and Data Governance Group; identify LGU authorities; approve a draft operating charter. | Signed charter, role matrix, initial meeting calendar. |
| Local baseline | Validate barangay list, population/service areas, center inventory, contact channels, devices, power backup, connectivity, staffing, and current DRRM workflows. | Baseline report with gaps and no-go constraints. |
| Community co-design | Conduct small, inclusive sessions in pilot barangays with women, PWDs, older persons, youth, farmers/fishers, responders, and health/social-welfare staff. | Prioritized resident journeys and accessibility findings. |
| Partner mapping | Map CSOs, NGOs, PRC, provincial agencies, schools, faith groups, and private-sector contributors; run due diligence. | Partner map and engagement shortlist. |
| Finance | Separate one-time equipment/training needs from recurring staffing, connectivity, power, maintenance, and drill costs. | Three-year cost categories and funding plan; no funding commitment assumed. |

**Gate 0: proceed only when** the LGU has named an accountable owner, approved a pilot boundary, confirmed a data-protection focal point, and accepted the baseline report.

### Phase 1 — LGU-only controlled pilot (Months 2–3)

**Objective.** Make the Command Center useful for municipal preparedness before connecting external partners.

| Activity | Pilot design |
|---|---|
| Core workflows | Verify alerts, update evacuation centers, run a mock SOS, acknowledge a record, and produce a handover note. |
| Deployment boundary | Use demo/training data or clearly labelled exercise data only; do not send public push messages or real emergency dispatches. |
| Staffing | Train a core MDRRMO group plus alternates; use a simple duty roster. |
| Barangay connection | Select a small number of representative pilot barangays through transparent criteria, not political convenience. |
| Evidence | Measure completion, handover quality, unresolved issues, usability, and accessibility; do not use unsupported claims of response-time improvement. |

**Gate 1: proceed only when** all core users can complete the exercise workflow, all open critical defects have owners, and the LGU can demonstrate a paper fallback if devices, power, or connectivity fail.

### Phase 2 — NGO and barangay readiness connection (Months 4–6)

**Objective.** Connect partners through limited, practical readiness workflows—not unrestricted platform access.

| Activity | Partner-safe starting point |
|---|---|
| Good Neighbors Philippines / anticipatory-action partner discussion | Explore trigger interpretation, inclusion of vulnerable groups, readiness checklists, and community drills. |
| PRC / health partner discussion | Explore first-aid training, referral protocol design, volunteer readiness, and non-identifiable capacity information. |
| Local CSO representation | Confirm accreditation/representation route, nominate focal persons, and conduct community feedback sessions. |
| Partner information exchange | Start with downloadable or view-only aggregated readiness reports, not system credentials. |
| MoUs and data agreements | Execute only after the scope, lawful basis, role, minimum data, and exit terms are accepted by the LGU. |

**Gate 2: proceed only when** every partner has a documented purpose, focal point, role matrix, safeguarding statement, and no unresolved data-sharing issue. The LGU must be able to disable partner access immediately if an agreement ends.

### Phase 3 — Supervised multi-agency exercises (Months 7–9)

**Objective.** Test the human system under realistic but controlled conditions.

| Exercise | What to test | Success evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Typhoon early-warning tabletop | Source verification, approval chain, center-readiness status, partner notification. | Each message has a source, approver, scope, and timestamp. |
| Offline SOS drill | Device outbox, SMS handoff, verified receipt simulation, duplicate suppression, resident-safe status. | Team distinguishes handoff, receipt, acknowledgement, and dispatch. |
| Evacuation-center readiness drill | Capacity update, barangay confirmation, accessibility needs, supply/resource requests. | Center changes are traceable and uncertainty is visible. |
| Partner coordination drill | NGO/CSO resource offer, approved referral, information sharing, and after-action review. | Partners receive only the data defined in their agreements. |

**Gate 3: proceed only when** after-action reviews show that staff can follow authority, privacy, and fallback rules under pressure. A successful drill is not enough if it exposes unresolved safeguarding or authority failures.

### Phase 4 — Limited live-readiness service (Months 10–12)

**Objective.** Move only approved, tested functions into a monitored operational posture.

| Function | Required preconditions |
|---|---|
| Verified public information display | Confirmed source policy, update ownership, accessibility review, and public correction process. |
| Live SMS gateway receipt | Approved provider, secret management, inbound verification, monitoring, duplicate handling, and LGU drill evidence. |
| Native push notifications | Consent workflow, device-token lifecycle, provider credentials, receipt handling, opt-out support, iOS/Android validation, and LGU release approval. |
| Partner dashboards | Valid MoU, role-based access, aggregation/minimum-data test, training, and deprovisioning procedure. |
| Live SOS workflow | Staffed response procedure, escalation contacts, operational duty roster, documented resident messaging, and continuous audit monitoring. |

**Gate 4: proceed only when** the LGU has formally approved the specific live function, not simply the platform in general. Each function should be activated separately and reversible if monitoring reveals a problem.

## 6. Economic and Resource Plan

The project should use a **municipal sustainability test** before accepting any grant-funded feature. The test asks: “Can Balangiga operate this safely after the grant, project staff, or vendor support ends?”

| Cost category | Low-cost starting approach | Sustainability control |
|---|---|---|
| Workstation and display | Reuse secure LGU devices where suitable; procure only necessary hardware after baseline. | Maintain an LGU asset register and replacement schedule. |
| Connectivity | Use existing LGU connection with documented backup and low-data workflows. | Test loss-of-connectivity procedures every quarter. |
| Power resilience | Inventory UPS/generator/charging capacity before buying new equipment. | Tie critical device plans to municipal power continuity arrangements. |
| Staffing | Integrate defined duties into MDRRMO and partner roles; avoid an unfunded new 24/7 post. | Publish duty roster and alternates; budget for refresher training. |
| Resident access | Keep basic mobile functions free; retain offline content and SMS handoff. | Do not introduce fees, mandatory data packages, or app-only public service. |
| NGO contributions | Accept training, equipment, or facilitation only through documented contributions. | Avoid a core service that stops when a single NGO project ends. |

The implementation team should present three budget scenarios to the LGU: **minimum viable pilot**, **operational readiness**, and **expanded multi-barangay service**. Each scenario should list one-time, recurring, in-kind, and contingency requirements separately. It should not use a generic national budget estimate because local procurement, existing assets, distance, power, and staffing conditions must first be verified.

## 7. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood to plan for | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership or staffing turnover | High in any long-lived municipal program | Written charter, alternates, role-based accounts, quarterly handover review. |
| Political or partner favoritism | Material trust risk | Published criteria, conflict declarations, neutral data rules, documented partner selection. |
| Funding gap after grant period | Material sustainability risk | Municipal cost baseline, phased activation, no single-donor dependency for core functions. |
| Connectivity or power outage | Expected during hazards | Offline mobile content, SOS outbox, SMS handoff, paper fallback, charging/power plan. |
| Misinformation or premature alerting | High public-safety risk | Source-verification SOP, approver log, correction protocol, public-status wording rules. |
| NGO data overreach | High privacy and trust risk | Data minimization, Tiered access, MoUs/DSAs, audit logs, periodic access review. |
| Uneven barangay participation | High inclusion risk | Transparent pilot criteria, rotating community sessions, inclusion quotas, feedback mechanisms. |
| Vendor lock-in | Long-term cost risk | LGU-owned accounts and asset register, exportable records, documented workflows, acceptance tests. |

## 8. Immediate 30-Day Action List

1. Issue a mayoral or authorized administrative call for a **Command Center inception meeting** with MDRRMO, planning, social welfare, health, budget, IT, barangay, and legal/privacy representatives.
2. Approve a **two-page project charter** stating purpose, pilot boundary, ownership, public-warning authority, dispatch authority, and no-live-send policy during the pilot.
3. Conduct the municipal baseline: staffing, contacts, evacuation centers, power, connectivity, current alert sources, mobile coverage, equipment, and barangay operating realities.
4. Nominate a data-protection focal person and prepare a first data inventory for alerts, centers, SOS, dispatch, audit, and partner data.
5. Publish transparent pilot-barangay selection criteria and schedule inclusive co-design sessions.
6. Prepare a partner map; invite Good Neighbors Philippines, Philippine Red Cross Eastern Samar Chapter, relevant provincial government offices, and locally accredited/eligible CSOs to separate discovery meetings.
7. Draft the partner role matrix and a standard MoU/data-sharing template before offering any dashboard or data access.
8. Run one no-send Command Center exercise using training data and record every gap in an after-action review.

---

## 9. Decision Requests for the Balangiga LGU

Before implementation begins, the LGU should make the following explicit decisions:

| Decision | Recommended answer for the pilot |
|---|---|
| Who owns the Command Center? | The Municipal DRRMO/MDRRMO, under LGU governance and documented authority. |
| Is the pilot live or training-only? | Training-only until function-specific operational gates are met. |
| Which barangays start first? | A small, representative set chosen through transparent hazard, access, and inclusion criteria. |
| Which partners can participate? | Partners with a clear mandate, due diligence result, defined role, and written agreement. |
| What data can partners see? | Aggregated readiness data by default; identifiable case data only under a lawful, documented necessity. |
| What does the LGU fund? | Core ownership, staffing, continuity, and basic operations; partner support may add capacity but not replace responsibility. |
| What triggers wider rollout? | Completed pilot gate, trained alternates, tested fallback processes, privacy compliance, accepted after-action review, and formal LGU approval. |
