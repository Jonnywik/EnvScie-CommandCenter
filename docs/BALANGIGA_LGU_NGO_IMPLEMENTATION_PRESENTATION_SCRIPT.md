# Speaker Script: Balangiga LGU and NGO Command Center Implementation Plan

**Audience.** Balangiga LGU leaders and staff, barangay representatives, NGOs/CSOs, humanitarian partners, and prospective technical or resource partners.

**Suggested duration.** 12–15 minutes, followed by 15–20 minutes for questions and decisions.

**Tone.** Clear, neutral, practical, and non-technical. The speaker should avoid promising faster rescue, guaranteed alerts, or live services that have not passed the LGU’s operational readiness gates.

> **Presenter reminder:** This presentation asks stakeholders to support a governed training-first rollout. It does not ask them to approve uncontrolled system access, live public alerts, or a technology project detached from local staffing and community needs.

---

## Slide 1 — Title: A Responsible Command Center Rollout for Balangiga

**Suggested visual.** Title, Balangiga location/context image or map, and the phrase “LGU-owned • Community-informed • Partner-supported.”

**Speaker notes.**

“Good [morning/afternoon]. Today we are presenting a practical way to introduce the Code for Resilience Command Center in Balangiga. The goal is not to build a large control room overnight. The goal is to create a small, reliable municipal capability that helps people organize verified information, prepare for hazards, coordinate responsibly, and learn from every exercise.

This plan keeps the Balangiga LGU in charge. NGOs, barangay groups, health and humanitarian partners, and private-sector supporters can contribute skills and resources, but they do not take over public-warning authority, dispatch authority, or access to residents’ private emergency records.

At the end of this presentation, we will ask for agreement on a 30-day training-first rollout, a partnership process, and the decisions needed before anything moves beyond the pilot.”

---

## Slide 2 — Why Balangiga Needs a Municipal-Scale Approach

**Suggested visual.** Simple context cards: population, multi-hazard exposure, access/economic constraints, community participation.

**Speaker notes.**

“Balangiga should not copy a city-scale emergency operations center. It needs a model that fits its actual size, capacity, and risks. The Philippine Statistics Authority lists Balangiga with a 2024 population of 14,473.[1] This tells us the starting point should be focused: a core LGU team, defined duty roles, a small pilot set of barangays, and practical fallback procedures.

The plan is also shaped by the realities of Eastern Samar. Balangiga is exposed to typhoons, storm surge, and flooding, and it has been identified among municipalities involved in regional anticipatory-action work.[2] At the same time, provincial poverty indicators remind us that we cannot design a service that assumes everyone has expensive phones, paid data, or stable home internet.[3]

So our principle is simple: low-cost resident access, offline content, an SMS handoff option, barangay-assisted participation, and a municipal operating model that can survive power, connectivity, and staffing gaps.”

---

## Slide 3 — What the Command Center Is, and What It Is Not

**Suggested visual.** Two-column table: “Supports” and “Does not automatically prove.”

**Speaker notes.**

“The Command Center supports verified information review, evacuation-center readiness, SOS workflow exercises, coordination logs, and structured handovers. It gives staff a shared picture of what is known, who is responsible, and what still needs verification.

But we must be very clear about its limits. A dashboard does not prove that a warning is approved. A message handoff does not prove that the LGU received it. A dispatch record does not prove that responders have arrived. A route preview does not prove that a road is safe.

This distinction protects both residents and responders. We will use plain language in the system and in public communications so that each status means exactly what it says.”

---

## Slide 4 — Governance: Who Is Accountable?

**Suggested visual.** Role diagram: executive sponsor, MDRRMO system owner, operations lead, public-information authority, privacy focal person, technical custodian, barangay liaison.

**Speaker notes.**

“The first implementation task is governance, not technology purchase. Republic Act No. 10121 emphasizes local capacity, accountability, transparency, and participation of civil society and communities in disaster risk reduction.[4]

For this rollout, we recommend a Command Center Steering and Data Governance Group. The Mayor or an authorized official serves as executive sponsor. The MDRRMO or Municipal DRRMO owns the operating workflow. A public-information authority approves public messages. A privacy focal person protects resident data. A technical custodian manages accounts and devices. Barangay liaisons connect the municipal process to real community conditions.

This role structure is based on positions and written procedures, not personal relationships. That is important because staff and leadership can change, but the system must remain understandable and accountable.”

---

## Slide 5 — How NGOs and CSOs Connect to the System

**Suggested visual.** Three partnership lanes: community readiness, humanitarian/health preparedness, and anticipatory action/resource coordination.

**Speaker notes.**

“We are not proposing one generic ‘NGO access’ account. Partners should be connected through specific jobs that solve specific local gaps.

Community groups can help validate evacuation-center information, identify accessibility barriers, support preparedness sessions, and report community feedback. Humanitarian and health partners can support first-aid training, referral design, volunteer preparedness, and readiness mapping. Anticipatory-action partners can help the LGU think through forecast triggers, vulnerable-group inclusion, and pre-agreed readiness actions.

Balangiga can begin with discovery meetings. Good Neighbors Philippines is a logical organization to invite because public reporting identifies Balangiga as a partner municipality in Eastern Samar anticipatory-action work.[2] The Philippine Red Cross Eastern Samar Chapter is also a relevant partner for first aid, emergency medical preparedness, referral planning, and volunteer readiness.[5]

The key message is this: a partner connection is a documented working relationship, not an automatic data connection.”

---

## Slide 6 — Protecting Residents’ Information and Trust

**Suggested visual.** Tiered access graphic: public, community readiness, partner coordination, LGU operations, technical administration.

**Speaker notes.**

“Trust is one of the most important resources in a disaster system. The Data Privacy Act requires transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality in personal-information processing.[6]

That means the LGU should remain the controller of Command Center data. By default, partners should receive only aggregated readiness information. They might see that several centers need accessibility checks, or that a training drill identified a communications gap. They should not automatically see a resident’s SOS record, location, health concern, or household details.

If there is a genuine need for an individual referral, the LGU will use a documented, minimum-data referral process with a named authorizer, secure transfer method, and retention date. No shared passwords. No screenshots in informal group chats. No open case lists. This is not only compliance—it is respect for residents.”

---

## Slide 7 — The Four-Phase Roadmap

**Suggested visual.** Four-phase timeline: mandate/baseline; LGU pilot; partner connection; supervised exercises; limited live readiness.

**Speaker notes.**

“The rollout is phased so that the system earns trust through evidence. First, we establish the mandate, baseline, and pilot boundary. Second, we run an LGU-only controlled pilot using training data. Third, we connect barangays and partners through readiness tasks and written agreements. Fourth, we conduct supervised exercises. Only after that do we consider limited live readiness for specific functions.

Each phase has a decision gate. We do not move forward because the calendar says so. We move forward only if we can show the right people, rules, access controls, fallback procedures, and after-action improvements are in place.

For example, native push notifications, live SMS receipt, partner dashboards, and live SOS workflows each require their own approvals. Approving the platform generally is not the same as approving every live feature.”

---

## Slide 8 — The First 30 Days: What We Will Actually Do

**Suggested visual.** Four weekly blocks: authority; baseline; community and partners; training and exercise; Day-30 gate.

**Speaker notes.**

“The first month is detailed in the 30-day checklist. In the first week, we confirm authority, named roles, pilot boundaries, and a training-only rule. In the second week, we verify real conditions: evacuation centers, connectivity, power, equipment, staffing, data categories, and current procedures.

In the third week, we conduct barangay and community sessions, map partners, and apply due diligence. We do not promise access or funding at this stage. In the fourth week, we configure training accounts, run role-based instruction, test paper fallback, and conduct a controlled scenario.

On Day 30, leadership makes one of three evidence-based decisions: proceed to a controlled pilot, pause for remediation, or reshape the initiative. That is a responsible decision point—not a ceremonial launch.”

---

## Slide 9 — Sustainability, Equity, and Political Safeguards

**Suggested visual.** Three columns: sustainable budget, equitable resident access, transparent institutional safeguards.

**Speaker notes.**

“A system is not sustainable if it only works while a grant, consultant, or single organization is present. We will separate one-time costs from recurring costs such as staffing, connectivity, power, maintenance, and refresher training. The LGU should be able to decide what it can operate safely after project support ends.

We also protect equity. Basic resident access should remain free. The plan keeps low-data and offline options because a disaster service should not exclude people who cannot afford expensive data or devices.

Finally, we protect institutional trust. Partner selection criteria should be published. Conflicts of interest should be declared. Public messages should be source-labelled and approved. The Command Center should never be used for partisan messaging or to give a private actor control over municipal emergency functions.”

---

## Slide 10 — Decisions Requested Today

**Suggested visual.** Six decision cards with checkboxes.

**Speaker notes.**

“We are asking the LGU and prospective partners to support six immediate decisions.

First, confirm that the MDRRMO or Municipal DRRMO will own the pilot under LGU governance. Second, approve the training-only boundary for the first 30 days. Third, designate the executive sponsor, operational leads, privacy focal person, and barangay liaison. Fourth, approve transparent criteria for selecting a small number of pilot barangays. Fifth, authorize discovery meetings with potential partners under the rule of no automatic access and no assumed commitment. Sixth, require that any MoU and any data-sharing arrangement receive legal and privacy review before signature.

These decisions give the team enough direction to begin responsibly without making promises that the LGU, the platform, or partners cannot yet support.”

---

## Slide 11 — Closing: Build Capacity Before Claiming Capability

**Suggested visual.** Closing statement: “LGU-owned. Community-informed. Partner-supported. Evidence-led.”

**Speaker notes.**

“The main opportunity is not simply to install a dashboard. It is to create a shared municipal practice: verify information, include communities, protect resident data, coordinate partners according to their real capabilities, and document decisions clearly.

If we follow the 30-day checklist, use the stakeholder matrix, and treat the MoU as a governed agreement rather than a shortcut to access, Balangiga can build a foundation that is useful during preparedness now and can grow responsibly over time.

Our closing principle is: **build capacity before claiming capability.** The next step is to confirm the first-month governance meeting, the named LGU roles, and the pilot boundary. Thank you. We welcome questions and practical concerns before we move to the Day-30 action plan.”

## Presenter Q&A Guide

| Likely question | Suggested answer |
|---|---|
| “Will this replace the MDRRMO or barangay response?” | “No. The Command Center supports the people and processes that already have lawful responsibility. It does not replace the MDRRMO, barangays, responders, or partners.” |
| “Can an NGO see all SOS requests?” | “No. Default partner information is aggregated. Individual information requires a defined service purpose, a lawful process, LGU approval, and minimum-data safeguards.” |
| “When can we send public alerts?” | “Only after the LGU has approved source verification, authority, provider configuration, accessibility, monitoring, and a function-specific release gate. The first 30 days are training-only.” |
| “Who pays for this after a grant ends?” | “The first-month baseline separates one-time from recurring costs. The LGU will decide what it can sustain before a function becomes essential.” |
| “Why not connect every partner immediately?” | “Because a useful partnership needs a clear role, safeguards, and accountability. Rapid unrestricted access can create privacy, misinformation, and authority risks.” |
| “Does the app guarantee rescue?” | “No. It helps record and coordinate a request. It must never claim that a handoff, receipt, acknowledgement, or dispatch is the same as responder arrival or a completed rescue.” |
